2024 to presentWith African Parks

Verifiable Nature Units and ecological integrity

An end-to-end software pipeline that automates the calculation of Verifiable Nature Units for biodiversity credits. Built in components with clear scope and daily stand-ups to catch mismatches early.

The ecological integrity explorer is an interactive map on top of the pipeline's outputs. It shows how ecological integrity changed between 2020 and 2025 per grid cell, and what drives it: habitat intactness and an indicator species score.

Pipeline complete; now being scaled to more protected areas.

KAZA TFCAGoogle Earth Engine

AutoML for land-cover classification

An in-house AutoML system for land-cover mapping in the Kavango-Zambezi landscape, built on Google Earth Engine. It searches for the best model and settings automatically, and does not rely on default configurations.

It maps nine land-cover classes across six areas (Zambezi, Mafunta, Mulobesi, Sichifulo, Hwange and Binga) for 2020 and 2023, and reports how certain each pixel's classification is.

Mean 10 to 27% improvement per class over default models (POPI metric).

Water pan siting

V02 prototype

A pipeline that identifies and ranks candidate sites for artificial water pans, balancing biophysical suitability, wildlife needs, cost and human-wildlife conflict risk. Around 29 variables are harmonised to a shared 100 m grid, normalised and combined into six category scores per 1 km² hexagon.

Seven scenarios scored in parallel, so decision makers can see how the ranking shifts with what is prioritised.

Farm field survey

A field data collection tool, co-developed and deployed with local communities in KAZA to map farm fields. It was built as part of the KAZA ARISE Bengo Global programme, which monitored how agroecology practices improve field sustainability, in cooperation with Digifarm.

Camera traps · Computer visionOpen source · MIT licence

Camera trap distance estimator

A small app that estimates how far an animal is from a camera trap. A depth model (Depth Anything) turns each image into a depth map, which is calibrated against a reference image of the same scene using points at known field distances.

In the animal image, one click on the subject lets the Segment Anything model outline it, and the app reports the calibrated distance within that outline together with an uncertainty estimate. It runs locally in the browser and can work offline once the models are downloaded.

Leiden University · Published 2022 · GIScience & Remote Sensing

AutoML for estimating grass height from Landsat

Can automated machine learning replace the trial-and-error an expert goes through when building a remote sensing model, and does it produce a better one?

1.73 – 1.78 cmtest error of the AutoML models
1.84 – 2.81 cmtest error of the classic, expert-style models
2AutoML systems compared: Auto-sklearn and AutoGluon

The problem

Grassland monitoring usually relies on a vegetation index such as NDVI and an empirical model trained on field data. Which bands, indices and model to use is typically settled by ad hoc experimentation, which does not guarantee the best possible model.

The data

Field measurements of grass height at the Oostvaardersplassen, a nature reserve in the Netherlands grazed by large herbivores, paired with Landsat ETM+ and OLI imagery.

The approach

We emulated the classic workflow exhaustively, testing every combination of spectral bands and vegetation indices with Bayesian optimisation for hyperparameter tuning. We then compared it against a naive use of two AutoML systems that select and tune the whole pipeline on their own.

The result

Both AutoML systems outperformed every classic configuration. In the classic models a vegetation index was always selected, but which one depended on the algorithm, which shows how fragile hand-picked choices are. The study covers a single site, so the numbers do not transfer directly to other areas.

Tools: Python, Auto-sklearn, AutoGluon, Bayesian optimisation, Landsat ETM+/OLIRead the paperWatch the talkESA feature with video interview
INVADER-IV, Coimbra · Published 2018 · Frontiers in Plant Science

Mapping the flowering of an invasive tree with drones

A biocontrol wasp was released in Portugal in 2015 to cut the flowering of Acacia longifolia. Could drone imagery track flowering over whole sites, where field counts are slow and cover only small plots?

7 sitesalong about 80 km of the Portuguese coast, around 65 ha each
4 – 6 cmimage resolution from a fixed-wing UAV
> 0.96overall accuracy at every site

The problem

The gall wasp Trichilogaster acaciaelongifoliae is expected to reduce flowering and so stop the seed bank from renewing. Monitoring that effect in the field is time-consuming, expensive and limited to small areas.

The data

Flights over five sand dune sites and two pine forest sites in March and April 2015, with a senseFly eBee carrying RGB and colour-infrared cameras. On the ground, at least 60 one-metre quadrats per site with flower counts, and nine or more ground control points.

The approach

Structure-from-motion produced orthophotos and a canopy height model. A Random Forest classifier then mapped flower presence from five inputs: red, green, blue, near-infrared and canopy height, with 1,000 reference points per site split 70/30 for training and validation.

The result

Flowering was mapped reliably in every habitat, with the blue band the most informative input at all sites but one. Estimating the number of flowers from mapped flower area did not work (r² between 0.013 and 0.156), because the plant's form and flower distribution vary too much. Peak flowering and open dunes gave the best results; pine canopy hides the understory.

Tools: senseFly eBee, Agisoft Photoscan, R (randomForest, raster), ArcGISRead the paper (open access)
INVADER-IV, Coimbra · Published 2019 · Biological Invasions

Citizen science to guide invasive plant surveillance

Researchers cannot survey every invasive plant everywhere. Can sightings reported by citizens improve distribution models and show where to look next?

3 speciesAcacia dealbata, A. longifolia and A. melanoxylon across Portugal
2,663citizen records from the Invasoras.pt platform
4,630records collected by researchers

The problem

Managing invasive plants depends on accurate and timely distribution data, which professionals alone cannot collect for all species and territories. Citizen science platforms could fill the gaps, if their data hold up in a model.

The data

Verified sightings from Invasoras.pt, the Portuguese citizen science platform for invasive plants, and occurrence data from researchers. From these we built three datasets per species: researchers only, citizens only, and both combined.

The approach

We modelled the potential distribution of each species with an ensemble of species distribution models (biomod2 in R), once per dataset. We then compared model accuracy, the environmental drivers each model selected and the predicted maps.

The result

All models reached very high accuracy, with the highest values from researcher data. Adding citizen data greatly increased the predicted distribution for every species. Ranking where the models agree but no records exist gave a map of surveillance priorities for citizens and researchers alike.

Tools: R, biomod2 ensemble modelling, Invasoras.pt citizen dataRead the paper
